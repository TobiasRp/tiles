# Tiles S2b to S4: attention, balance, and two probes that said no

Fourth status record for the blog series. It follows
[the S3 post](s3-weight-prefetch-2026-10.md) and covers four milestones of
[the megakernel plan](../megakernel_next_steps.md): S2b (attention at the
right parallelism), S2c (balanced QKV), S6a (a controlled measurement
protocol) and S4 (fewer wave boundaries). Everything was measured on `tux`,
an RTX 3050 8 GB, between 2026-10-25 and 2026-10-26. The plan's Sections 11
to 18 hold the full tables; raw artifacts are in `runs/s2b-*`, `runs/s2c-*`,
`runs/s6a-*` and `runs/s4-*` on tux.

## In one paragraph

After S2, TileInfer ran Llama 3.2 1B at batch one about as fast as PyTorch's
CUDA Graph replay at short context and 15% faster at 4K, 1.3 ms above the
bandwidth floor. Four milestones later it takes **12.11 ms per token at
context 1 and 12.98 ms at 4K**, 0.50 ms above the 11.61 ms floor, **5.7%
faster than graph replay at context 1 and 36% faster at 4K**, and 9 to 11%
faster than llama.cpp's fastest configuration. Almost all of the gain came
from two changes that needed no new synchronization: attention rewritten
around memory-level parallelism (S2b), and QKV rows split evenly over the
workers (S2c). S4 fused each worker's FFN and saved another 70 µs. Two
probes said no: weight prefetch across barriers (S3, the previous post) and
counter waits in place of the barriers around attention (S4). Both were
cheap to run and each saved a package-format change.

## Where we started

The S2 trace at context 1 split the 1.3 ms gap to the floor into GEMV
streaming below the roofline (0.72 ms), attention (0.42 ms) and barrier
latency (0.13 ms). At 4K, attention alone lost 4 ms per token: it reached
14 GB/s against a 213 GB/s roofline.

| Milestone | Context 1, ms | 4K, ms | How measured |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| S2 | 12.90 | 17.22 | exploratory, unlocked clocks |
| S2b attention | 12.32 | 13.16 | exploratory, same-session S2 rerun |
| S2c balanced QKV | 12.189 | 13.050 | controlled (S6a), locked clocks |
| S4 fused FFN | 12.114 | 12.979 | controlled, both spawn orders |

Exploratory numbers from one session cannot resolve the 0.1 ms steps of the
later milestones; the controlled rows can (see S6a below).

## S2b: attention is a memory-parallelism problem

The plan blamed S2's attention on its block reductions. A probe on the
worker's own launch shape (`attention_probe`) showed otherwise:

- **Short context was a chain of dependent global round trips.** Each task
  loaded `q` and K, stored scores, reloaded them for the statistics, stored
  those, and the values task reloaded them again: about 7 µs per task, and
  40 tasks on 20 workers ran two in a row. The warp reductions cost 1 µs per
  layer.
- **Long context kept one row in flight.** Each warp loaded one 128-byte K
  row at a time with dependent 2-byte loads. A plain row-read kernel with
  16-byte loads reaches 169 GB/s on the same cache layout, so no layout
  change was needed.

The rewrite gives each lane 16-byte K/V loads with two rows in flight, keeps
`q` in registers, and runs **one task per worker**: one chunk per K/V head up
to 384 steps, two beyond, so 8 or 16 tasks on 20 workers. Up to 384 steps the
task is *fused*: it keeps scores and probabilities in shared memory, finishes
softmax and P·V itself, and the barrier between the two attention waves is
skipped on every worker (a *conditional barrier* the validator checks in both
regimes).

Attention excess fell from 430 to 104 µs per token at context 1 and from
4,047 to 284 µs at 4K. TileInfer became faster than graph replay at every
context: −4% at context 1, −35% at 4K.

One cost was not in the plan. The first general version instantiated the
attention tasks for every group and lane count; the worker grew from 8,824 to
89,248 SASS instructions, and *unrelated* GEMV phases slowed by up to
85 µs per token although only one instantiation ever ran. Moving the general
shapes out of line was worse (14.3 ms per token). The final worker has one
compile-time instantiation for the model's shape and one general path, 17,816
instructions. In a persistent kernel every opcode shares one register
allocation and one instruction cache.

## S2c: the largest loss was a balance problem

After S2b the biggest single loss was not a barrier. QKV tasks owned one
whole head each, so that RoPE and the K cache write fit in the task: 48 tasks
on 20 workers in rounds of 3 and 2. The last worker finished 138 µs per token
after the average one.

S2c splits the 3,072 rows of `[Q; K; V]` into 20 near-equal ranges with one
norm prologue each and moves RoPE into the attention task, which already
owns its K/V head's four query heads and is the only reader of the current K
row. The arithmetic does not change, and **outputs are bit-identical to S2b**
for float32, BF16 and the checkpoint, in both attention regimes. QKV excess
fell from 202 to 75 µs per token; the token got 0.10 to 0.13 ms faster at
every context.

## S6a: measuring a tenth of a millisecond

S2c and S4 each aimed at about 0.1 ms, 0.8% of a token, while repeated
exploratory runs differed by up to 0.5%. S6a built a protocol before more
milestones were judged:

- **Resident arms.** Each package loads once into a server process that
  answers fixed-position benchmark requests, so arms alternate in blocks
  without reloading 2.5 GB.
- **Alternating, paired blocks** in forward and reversed order, with a
  bootstrap interval over paired differences and GPU telemetry per block.
- **A/A runs** of one package against itself: **about 10 µs per token
  (0.08%) is resolvable in one session, about 5 µs with both spawn orders.**

Two things surfaced. The second-spawned server is 1 to 8 µs faster than the
first in every session, with the same binary and package; it is not
explained, and every comparison since runs both orders, which cancels it.
And hashing the 2.5 GB weights took 19 of the 20.5 s package load, so
measurement modes now trust the declared checksum after a one-time
`tileplan verify`. A gate comparison went from 220 s to 42 s.

Under the protocol S2c was 0.10 to 0.13 ms faster than S2b, which agreed with
the device trace and not with an earlier unpaired sweep that had shown half
that.

## S4: one fusion pays, one synchronization scheme does not

S4 was planned around removing three of the five barriers per layer. After
S2b and S2c its ceiling was about 0.15 ms, so it opened with two probes.

### The fused FFN

Gate/up waited for its last worker before down could start. In the fused
form, worker *k* computes the gate/up rows of product chunk *k* and then that
chunk's FP32 down partial over all 2,048 outputs, from data it produced
itself. After a barrier, each worker sums the 20 partials for its slice of
the layer output in fixed order (no float atomics), rounds, and adds the
residual, at the same BF16 rounding boundaries as before.

`bandwidth_probe ffn` projected **0.22 ms per token**, with two findings:
storing `Wdown` transposed, so a worker's columns are contiguous, was worth
as much as the fusion itself, and having every next-layer worker repeat the
full 20-partial reduction cost 4.6 µs per layer (3.2 MB through L2), more
than one slice-sized reduction plus a barrier.

Built into the decoder (package format 9), it saves **70 to 75 µs per token
at every context**, ±2 µs, in both spawn orders. Every calibrated checkpoint
tolerance passes, slightly closer to PyTorch than before (logits RMS error
0.0274 against 0.0298); outputs are no longer bit-identical to S2c because the
FP32 summation order changed, and one 64-token generation diverges at a
one-unit tie.

The gain is a third of the projection. The fused waves stream at 209.7 GB/s,
`lm_head`'s rate, as the probe showed. But the probe's own split baseline ran
24.7 µs per layer above ideal where the decoder's FFN ran 16, so there was
less to win, and the reduction wave cost 3.2 µs per layer in the decoder
against 2 in the probe. **A probe's baseline has to be the decoder's own
phase, not a reimplementation of it.**

### Counters around attention

The second probe took that lesson literally. `counter_probe` loads the real
checkpoint package and runs its validated instruction streams with a copy of
the worker's dispatch loop over the same operators; its barrier mode times
within 0.3% of the decoder. Modes then drop the barriers before attention,
between its waves and before Wo, and each task after a dropped barrier waits
on a counter shared by all tasks with the same predecessors (release adds,
acquire spins, targets of launches × producers, so no resets). Logits stay
bit-identical in every mode, also with random delays before every task.

| Context | QKV → attention | + attention → Wo | all three |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | −4 µs | −14 µs | −13 µs |
| 385 | −5 µs | +3 µs | +10 µs |
| 1024 | −0 µs | +6 µs | +15 µs |
| 4096 | −0 µs | +1 µs | +4 µs |

Positive is faster. The gate was 60 µs. A first version that polled one flag
per predecessor was 96 µs *slower*: Wo waits for 16 tasks.

It does not pay because there is nothing to overlap. After S2c, QKV's
producers finish within about 2 µs per layer of each other, and Wo reads the
whole attention vector, so it waits for the last attention task whether a
barrier or a counter tells it. A counter's add and acquire cost about what
the barrier did. Splitting Wo by K/V group would be the only way to overlap
it with attention, and the traces price that below the gate once its own
reduction is counted. The counter executor, which was the centerpiece of the
original plan, is not built.

## Where the last 0.50 ms goes

The S4 trace at context 1, µs per token beyond ideal streaming:

| Loss | µs | Note |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| FFN streaming excess | 97 | fused waves at 209.7 GB/s |
| QKV streaming excess | 84 | one norm prologue per wave, pipeline ramp |
| Wo streaming excess | 50 | |
| `lm_head` streaming excess | 46 | one long wave at 209 GB/s |
| FFN reduction wave | 37 | plus 14 µs of its barrier |
| Barrier latency | 92 | about 82 barriers, 16 of them skipped when attention is fused |
| Attention | 96 | 8 fused tasks per layer while 12 workers idle |

Streaming excess is spread over every wave and is mostly the ramp after each
barrier and the last few percent of the roofline. No single remaining item is
worth more than about 0.1 ms, and the two mechanisms that targeted the
barriers directly (prefetch and counters) were measured to gain nothing on
this GPU.

## What we learned

1. **Measure the mechanism before the format.** Every kernel milestone
   since S3 opened with a probe gate stated in µs per token. Two of the five
   gates failed (S3's prefetch and S4's counters), and each failure cost
   hours instead of a package format.
2. **Probe on the decoder's own code and package.** The FFN probe's
   reimplemented baseline overstated the gain by a factor of three; the
   counter probe ran the real streams and was right.
3. **Balance and memory-level parallelism beat clever synchronization.**
   The big wins (S2b, S2c) gave every worker one task with enough bytes in
   flight. Barriers cost about 1 µs each here, and once work is balanced
   there is little behind them to recover.
4. **In a persistent kernel, code size is a performance property.** One
   opcode's template explosion slowed every other opcode.
5. **A 0.1 ms claim needs a protocol.** Paired, alternating blocks with both
   spawn orders resolve 5 µs; unpaired sweeps disagreed with the trace by a
   factor of two.

## What is next

in one launch; it should barely change ms per token but completes the single

## How these numbers were produced

From `tux:~/dev/tiles`, with the packages from `tileplan import-llama32`:

```sh
cmake --build tileinfer/build/release
tileinfer/build/release/attention_probe              # S2b gate
tileinfer/build/release/bandwidth_probe qkv          # S2c gate
tileinfer/build/release/bandwidth_probe ffn          # S4 FFN gate
tileinfer/build/release/counter_probe PACKAGE        # S4 counter gate
bash runs/s4-impl-2026-10-26/timing.sh               # controlled S2c vs S4, traces
```
